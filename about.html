About

I've never been able to pick just one thing.

They make considerably more sense when you know what I've been paying attention to. This is the long version of how a Money Behavior Strategist, a nonprofit founder, and a rootworker turned out to be the same person.

The thread underneath it

I kept seeing the same kind of gap.

I saw children who had been taught what to do with hygiene and menstrual care — and still didn't always do it. I saw credit clients who knew the textbook advice, repaired their credit, and later found themselves back in debt. I saw people know exactly what they intended to do with money before it arrived and then do something different once it was there.

And I couldn't pretend I was standing outside of it. I've had money set aside for one thing and chosen to use it for something else because somebody asked, something came up, or I wanted something badly enough in the moment.

That made me suspicious of the idea that information alone changes behavior. If someone already knows better, knowing isn't necessarily the missing piece. So what is?

That's the question I kept following.

How I tend to look at things

Get specific before you settle for the story.

I'm less interested in deciding what's wrong with somebody than understanding what's actually happening. A behavior can make sense without being helpful. Understanding a decision doesn't require approving it. Knowing better doesn't guarantee doing better. And calling somebody lazy, irresponsible, difficult, undisciplined, or broken usually tells me less than asking what happened.

So I pay attention. When does it happen? When doesn't it? How do you know? What changed? What's missing from the explanation? What already works? What would a useful next move actually look like?

Sometimes clarity changes the behavior. Sometimes accountability is needed. Sometimes circumstances have to change. And sometimes we've been trying to solve the wrong problem. That's usually where things get interesting.

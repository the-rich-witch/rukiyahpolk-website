Paying attention is a skill, not a personality trait

Most money problems aren't math problems. Here's what actually gets missed, and why.

Somebody tells me they "just aren't good with money," and I always want to ask what they think that means. Because in eleven years of doing this work, I have never once met someone who couldn't do the math. Everyone can subtract. What people actually struggle with is noticing — in real time, before the money is gone — what's happening.

That's a different skill than arithmetic. It's closer to the kind of attention you'd bring to reading a room, or reading a person. And like any skill, it can be dulled by exhaustion, by scarcity, by growing up in a house where money was never discussed out loud except as a crisis. It can also be built back up. But you can't build up a skill you've been told is a character flaw.

The story people tell themselves

"I'm bad with money" is a tidy explanation. It closes the conversation. It also happens to be wrong almost every time I hear it, because it skips the actual sequence of events. Something happened. A decision got made, often fast, often under pressure, often for a reason that made complete sense in the moment. The label shows up afterward, to explain the outcome without having to look at the decision.

My work starts by refusing the label and going back for the sequence. What did you plan to do. What actually happened. What got in the way. That's it — that's the whole method, before we ever get to a spreadsheet.

Why this matters more than the plan itself

You can hand almost anyone a good budget. Budgets are not rare. What's rare is a plan that survives contact with a Tuesday — with the flat tire, the invitation you didn't want to decline, the version of you that's tired and just wants today to be easier. A plan built without attention to those moments isn't a plan. It's a wish with a spreadsheet attached.

So when I sit down with someone, the first job isn't fixing the plan. It's building the attention that lets them notice, next time, that the moment is happening — while there's still a choice in it.

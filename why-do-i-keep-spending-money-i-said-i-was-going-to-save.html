Why Do I Keep Spending Money I Said I Was Going to Save?

Rukiyah Polk, Money Behavior Strategist

See It ✦ Ask Why ✦ Try Something New ✦ Make a Plan

You get paid.

You move some money into savings.

This time, you mean it.

I'm not touching this money.

Then something happens. Sometimes nothing big happens at all.

A few days later, you're opening your banking app and moving some of that money right back into checking.

And you're thinking: "I literally told myself I wasn't touching that."

So what happened?

That's a better question than: "Why can't I save money?"

Because you did save it. The question is what happened between deciding to save the money and deciding to spend it.

The part we usually skip

We tend to look at money choices like there are only two moments: I said I was going to save it. Then: I spent it.

That makes it easy to decide the problem must be discipline.

But there's a whole middle part we're missing.

You moved $200 into savings on payday, then realized you didn't leave enough for groceries. Your checking account dropped lower than you were comfortable seeing. Somebody asked you for money. You had been telling yourself "no" all week, saw something you really wanted, and finally said, "Forget it." Or you looked at the $1,000 you'd built up and $50 suddenly didn't seem like much. Sometimes you truly needed the money. Other times, the account was called "savings," but the money didn't have a clear purpose yet.

Those situations might look the same on a bank statement. Money went into savings. Money came back out. But they aren't the same problem. And they probably don't need the same solution.

Look at what happened in the middle

Think of your money choice like crossing a bridge.

On one side, you decided: I'm saving this money. On the other side, you decided: I'm spending some of it.

Something happened between those two decisions. That's the part worth studying.

Instead of asking, "What's wrong with me? Why do I always do this?" try asking:

"What changed between the moment I decided to save this money and the moment I decided to spend it?"

Don't rush to find a deep answer. Start with what actually happened.

What were you buying? What happened that day? How much was left in checking? What did you tell yourself before you moved the money? Was the expense unexpected? Did taking the money solve a real problem?

You aren't trying to put yourself on trial. You're collecting information.

Sometimes the plan is the problem

Let's say you get paid $1,500. You immediately move $400 into savings because you're serious about getting your money together.

That sounds responsible.

But after paying bills, buying groceries, getting gas, and handling everything else that needs to happen before payday, you're short. So you take $150 back out of savings.

You could tell yourself, "See? I can't save." But that's not the only explanation.

You might have created a plan that required you to live on less money than your life actually costs.

If this happens again and again, trying harder may not fix it.

The plan may need to change.

Saving $250 and leaving it there may work better than moving $400 and repeatedly pulling $150 back.

The goal isn't to make the plan look good on payday. The goal is to make a plan that still works after payday.

And sometimes savings is supposed to be spent

Taking money out of savings doesn't automatically mean something went wrong.

If your tire blows and you use the money you saved for emergencies, the savings did its job.

If you've been putting aside money for your car registration and then use it to pay your registration, that's not breaking your savings plan. That's completing it.

The useful question isn't, "Did I touch my savings?" It's: "Did I use this money for what I intended — or did something change?"

"Savings" may be too vague

Imagine you have $600 sitting in an account labeled Savings.

Now imagine that same $600 is labeled Car Registration – October.

Those can feel very different.

"Savings" doesn't tell you what the money is doing. It may start to look like extra money that happens to be sitting somewhere else.

But money with a specific job gives you something to compare the new purchase against.

Here's a question worth asking: Is this money actually saved for something, or is it money I haven't spent yet?

There isn't a right answer. You just want to know which one is true.

Try this before you move the money

The next time you're about to transfer money out of savings, don't immediately tell yourself no. Pause. Ask: "What changed since I decided to save this?"

That's it.

The point isn't to force yourself to keep the money in savings. The point is to understand the decision you're making.

Recalculate. Don't throw away the whole plan.

Think about using GPS.

You make a wrong turn and the GPS doesn't say, "Well, you ruined everything. Good luck." It finds where you are now and gives you another route.

Your money plan can work the same way.

If something didn't work, you don't have to abandon the whole thing.

Look at what happened. Figure out why the choice made sense at the time. Try something different. Then adjust the plan.
